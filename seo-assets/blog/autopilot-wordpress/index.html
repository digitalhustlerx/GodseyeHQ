Run WordPress on autopilot: the small-site workflow

Field notes from the Godseye team · 5 min read Guide

Here's the pattern most small-site owners fall into: you launch, it goes great for a week, then the upkeep kicks in. A broken image here, a stale sale page there, a plugin update that turns your homepage into a white screen. Each fix is a trip back into wp-admin — or a message to a developer who bills you for every single task.

It doesn't have to be that way. The routine work of running a WordPress site can be handled in the same place you already do everything else: a chat.

The real cost of "just maintaining" WordPress

WordPress isn't hard to use once — it's hard to keep using. The overhead shows up in a thousand small decisions: did I update the plugins? who fixed the footer? which post did I schedule for Tuesday? For a solo operator or a small team, that's hours every week that came out of actually building the business.

The math most owners don't do: a developer charging $50 per small task, at 3–4 tasks a month, is $150–200 in pure upkeep — before anything actually grows the site. The "cheap fix" is usually not the fix, it's just the next invoice.

What "autopilot" actually looks like day to day

Autopilot is not "the site runs itself forever." It's: when a task shows up, you don't go hunting for the right tool — you just ask. The routine jobs become chat messages instead of dashboards:

  • Publish & edit posts — "draft a post about our new sale" or "fix the typo in the homepage headline"
  • Store ops (WooCommerce) — "how many orders need shipping?" or "create a 10% discount code"
  • Media & fixes — "the product image on the Shop page is broken, replace it"
  • Comments & moderation — "approve the comments on yesterday's post, delete the spam"
  • Site health & security — "run a security sweep, tell me what looks off"
  • Scheduled work — "post this every Monday at 9am"

Each of those is a command you type in plain English. The site owner stays in charge — nothing ships without you asking — but they stop being the one who has to know how to do it.

Why chat beats a dashboard

Dashboards are great at showing you things. They're bad at doing them, because every action means clicking through menus, remembering where a setting lives, and translating what you want into the interface's vocabulary. Chat flips that: you say what you want, in the same words you'd say to a colleague.

That also means the learning curve is flat. There's no admin panel to learn, no plugin hierarchy to memorize, no "which menu was that under again?" A new team member picks it up in minutes because they already know how to send a message.

Keep the two things that still matter

Autopilot isn't handing over control. Two guardrails stay in place:

  • You approve before anything ships. The agent proposes; you confirm. No surprise changes on a live site.
  • You own the site and the content. Your WordPress install, your posts, your data. The automation sits on top, it never replaces your WordPress.
